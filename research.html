A New Quantum Monte Carlo for Generic Lattice Hamiltonians

Duke University Prof. Shailesh Chandrasekharan May 2024 – Present

Quantum Monte Carlo methods are among the most powerful tools for studying quantum many-body systems, but frustrated models generically suffer from the sign problem, an NP-hard obstruction to reliable sampling arising from cancellations of positive and negative weights. Even where the ground state is accessible, obtaining excited states reliably remains an open challenge.

Working with Prof. Chandrasekharan, I developed the Projected Density Matrix Sampling (PDMS) method: a continuous-time path-integral Monte Carlo approach for computing low-lying spectra of generic lattice Hamiltonians. Rather than tracing the full thermal density matrix, PDMS samples within a carefully chosen projection subspace while maintaining ergodicity and detailed balance. The method achieves rapid convergence in sign-free systems and yields spectral information even in the presence of sign problems. We applied it to the Shastry–Sutherland model and to the 1D Transverse Field Ising Model near the E₈ critical point, obtaining scaling relations and low-lying energies previously inaccessible to Monte Carlo. Results are in a first-author paper currently under review at Physical Review D.

Current Work

I am currently designing a second-generation PDMS algorithm for the Shastry–Sutherland model incorporating two targeted changes: (i) replacing the conventional Sz basis with the orthogonal-dimer basis, which is better adapted to the model's frustrated geometry, and (ii) using physically motivated projection subspaces designed to capture the distinct phases of the SSM phase diagram.

Quantum Materials Synthesis and Characterization

Duke University Prof. Sara Haravifard Aug 2022 – Present

I joined the Haravifard Lab in my first year to work on frustrated quantum materials. For spinel compounds (Cr-doped ZnAl₂O₄ and Li-doped ZnV₂O₄), I synthesized phase-pure samples via solid-state techniques and used high-resolution XRD with Rietveld refinement in FullProf to determine lattice parameters across dopings and quantify deviations from Vegard's law. I also performed arc melting on TmB₄ and HoB₄, metallic altermagnet candidates related to the Shastry–Sutherland lattice.

For perovskites, I synthesized phase-pure NdFeO₃, a theoretical topological magnon candidate, and grew a single crystal using the optical floating zone furnace. I performed Laue scattering for orientation alignment and PPMS measurements to obtain temperature-dependent magnetization and hysteresis curves. Preliminary results were presented at APS 2025; the crystal is currently being re-grown for inelastic neutron scattering studies.

Atmospheric Pollution and Climate Physics

Duke University Prof. Brian G. McAdoo Nov 2022 – Present

Working with Prof. McAdoo's group, I modeled transboundary PM₂.₅ dynamics in Nepal and the Indo-Gangetic Plain using HYSPLIT back-trajectory analysis, aerosol optical depth (AOD), and potential source contribution functions (PSCF). On the local scale, I examined diurnal PM₂.₅ patterns, planetary boundary layer height (PBLH), and ventilation coefficients to isolate local and non-local pollution sources. This work contributed to a co-first-author paper now published in Atmospheric Pollution Research.

Collider Physics for HL-LHC Upgrade

Duke University Prof. Ashutosh Kotwal Apr 2023 – Aug 2023

As a Huang Fellow (2023), I designed track reconstruction algorithms for the HL-LHC upgrade with Prof. Kotwal's group. I implemented a wedge generation algorithm that segments the transverse and longitudinal sections of concentric silicon detectors, and a spacepoint divider that assigns collision data to those wedges, enabling parallelized, real-time silicon track-trigger reconstruction for theorized metastable dark matter candidates. Contributions were acknowledged in a published paper in Scientific Reports.
